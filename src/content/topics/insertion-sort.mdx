You're dealt cards one at a time. Your hand is always sorted: each new card slides left past the bigger ones until it finds its spot. You never re-sort the whole hand. That's insertion sort.

### The idea

- The part `a[0..i-1]` is always **sorted**.
- Take `key = a[i]`.
- Walk left from `i - 1`, **shifting** every item bigger than `key` one place right.
- When you hit an item ≤ key (or the start), drop `key` into the gap.

### Why it's adaptive

If the array is already sorted, every key is ≥ its left neighbour, so the while loop stops at once: **n − 1 comparisons, O(n)**. If it's reversed, every key travels all the way to the front: **n(n−1)/2**, O(n²). The less "out of order" the data, the faster it runs.

### Shift, don't swap

Each step of the while loop is **one assignment** (`a[j+1] = a[j]`), not a three-assignment swap. That's why insertion sort usually beats bubble sort in practice, even though both are O(n²).
